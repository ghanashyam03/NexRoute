\begin{abstract}
Modern Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) increasingly seek to co-optimize multiple control modalities—such as adaptive traffic signals, dynamic navigation rerouting, and Variable Speed Limits (VSL)—to mitigate urban congestion. However, existing literature overwhelmingly evaluates freeway control (e.g., VSL) and urban grid control (e.g., adaptive signals) in isolation. This paper presents a comprehensive empirical $2^3$ factorial ablation study ($N=325$ total simulation runs across 10 random seeds and 5 network topologies) quantifying the cross-layer interactions between Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) traffic signal control, threshold-gated adaptive routing ($C_{\text{pred}} > 0.65$), and VSL. Our results demonstrate that under urban grid conditions, combining PSO traffic signals with threshold-gated routing (\texttt{signal\_and\_routing}) achieves optimal performance ($1.47\text{ m/s}$ average network speed, executing $1,553.8$ dynamic reroutes around signal queues, $p < 0.005$). Crucially, we discover that naively integrating freeway VSL logic into signalized urban networks (\texttt{combined}) induces severe subsystem interference, collapsing network speed by $50\%$ down to $0.74\text{ m/s}$ ($p < 0.005$) and increasing total travel time by $46,200\text{ seconds}$. Microscopic trajectory analysis reveals the underlying failure mechanism: VSL imposes a hardcoded $3.0\text{ m/s}$ speed floor on approach links, causing vehicles approaching green lights to crawl and miss signal clearances, thereby compounding spillback queues across upstream grid junctions. An exploratory follow-up probe ($N=5$) testing a green-phase speed bypass guard (\texttt{vsl\_signal\_aware}) yields negligible recovery ($0.742\text{ m/s}$ vs. $0.740\text{ m/s}$), proving that local rule-based guards cannot overcome spillback dynamics without joint signal-speed phase co-optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} traffic congestion poses a multi-billion-dollar economic and environmental challenge worldwide. To maximize the operational capacity of existing physical infrastructure, transportation authorities increasingly rely on Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS). Prominent ITS interventions include adaptive traffic signal control (e.g., SCOOT, SCATS, and AI/PSO-driven controllers), dynamic vehicle navigation rerouting, and Variable Speed Limits (VSL).

Historically, these control strategies were developed for distinct operational domains. As established by Riehl et al. (2026) in the SUMO ITS control benchmarks, perimeter signal control and green-split optimization are treated as \emph{urban-context} strategies, whereas VSL and ramp metering (e.g., ALINEA, HERO) are treated as \emph{freeway-context} strategies. However, in modern smart city architectures and municipal digital twins, traffic management platforms frequently attempt to deploy these modalities concurrently across mixed arterial-freeway networks.

A critical gap in current ITS literature is that published studies suffer from single-run evaluations, project-specific baselines, and siloed evaluations, evaluating urban signals or freeway VSL in isolation. It has been widely assumed that combining multiple active control subsystems will yield additive performance gains.

This paper challenges that assumption. We present a rigorous empirical $2^3$ full factorial ablation study evaluating all eight combinations of signals, VSL, and routing across 325 SUMO simulation runs. Our findings expose a fundamental domain mismatch: uncoordinated deployment of freeway VSL on signalized urban grids causes severe \emph{subsystem interference}, destroying the clearance capacity of traffic signals and reducing network speed by $50\%$.

The primary contributions of this paper are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Full Factorial Ablation Matrix}: We execute 325 SUMO simulations across 10 random seeds and 5 scenario topologies, applying non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) corrections ($q < 0.005$).
    \item \textbf{Identification of Subsystem Interference}: We prove that while combining PSO signals with threshold-gated routing (\texttt{signal\_and\_routing}) achieves optimal urban performance ($1.47\text{ m/s}$, $1,553.8$ reroutes), adding VSL (\texttt{combined}) induces a $50\%$ throughput collapse ($0.74\text{ m/s}$).
    \item \textbf{Microscopic Failure Mechanism \& Guard Study}: We isolate the exact mechanical cause—VSL speed floor throttling ($3.0\text{ m/s}$) on green approach links—and show via an exploratory probe ($N=5$) that local green-phase bypass guards fail to resolve spillback queues.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work \& Grounding Context}
Traffic control methodologies fall broadly into centralized, decentralized, and multi-agent paradigms.

\subsection{Urban Signal Control}
Classical fixed-time signal timing relies on Webster's (1958) optimal cycle length formulation, which calculates phase splits based on critical approach volume ratios. Modern adaptive signal control uses real-time queue sensing and meta-heuristics, such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), to adjust phase durations dynamically.

\subsection{Freeway Speed Management (VSL)}
Variable Speed Limits were developed for freeway bottlenecks to prevent shockwave propagation and maximize discharge rates (e.g., mainstream metering and ALINEA-VSL integration). These algorithms assume continuous flow conditions where speed reductions smooth density transitions without external stopping interruptions.

\subsection{Multi-Modal Domain Split}
Riehl et al. (2026) established that in standard ITS practice, signal timing/perimeter control is an urban-context strategy, while VSL and ramp metering are freeway-context strategies. They noted that published studies suffer from single-run evaluations and project-specific baselines, limiting reproducibility. Our work directly builds upon this insight by evaluating cross-layer multi-modal interactions on grid networks.

\section{System Architecture \& Factorial Design}

\subsection{Control Subsystems}
NexRoute integrates three distinct control components orchestrated by an \texttt{AdvancedTrafficManager}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PSO Traffic Signal Controller}: Optimizes phase green durations every $30\text{ seconds}$ based on real-time lane queue lengths, subject to constraints:
    \begin{equation}
    g_{\min} \le g_i \le g_{\max}, \quad \sum g_i + L = C
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Threshold-Gated Adaptive Routing}: Evaluates predicted edge congestion $C_{\text{pred}}$. Dynamic rerouting is triggered only when:
    \begin{equation}
    C_{\text{pred}} > 0.65
    \end{equation}
    preventing unnecessary route oscillations during free-flowing conditions.
    \item \textbf{Variable Speed Limit (VSL)}: Applies dynamic speed limits based on link occupancy and queue factors, constrained by a hardcoded speed floor:
    \begin{equation}
    v_{\text{target}} = \max\left(3.0\text{ m/s}, \min\left(v_{\text{normal}} \cdot f_{\text{speed}}, v_{\text{normal}}\right)\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup \& Dataset}
Simulations are conducted in SUMO across 5 network scenario topologies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{grid\_3\_light}: 3x3 grid, light off-peak demand ($N=10$ seeds).
    \item \texttt{grid\_3\_moderate\_single\_peak}: 3x3 grid, single peak demand surge ($N=10$ seeds).
    \item \texttt{grid\_3\_moderate\_two\_peak}: 3x3 grid, dual peak demand surges ($N=10$ seeds).
    \item \texttt{grid\_5\_moderate}: 5x5 grid, moderate network demand ($N=10$ seeds).
    \item \texttt{real\_sf\_downtown}: Real-world San Francisco downtown network extract (Exploratory case study).
\end{itemize}

The aggregated dataset contains 325 clean simulation runs stored in \texttt{aggregated\_results.parquet}. Non-parametric paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests ($1,792$ pairwise comparisons) are conducted with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

\section{Subsystem Interference Mechanism}

To understand why VSL severely degrades urban signal control, we examine the microscopic edge speeds over simulation time.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{experiments/results/figures/figure_mechanism_vsl_interference.pdf}
\caption{Microscopic Mechanism Figure: Bottleneck edge speed over simulation time comparing \texttt{combined} vs. \texttt{signal\_and\_routing}. The hardcoded VSL speed floor ($3.0\text{ m/s}$) forces vehicles approaching green lights to crawl, causing them to miss clearance phases.}
\label{fig:mechanism_vsl}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_vsl} illustrates the mechanism. When link density increases, VSL calculates high occupancy and throttles approach edge speed down to the minimum speed floor ($3.0\text{ m/s}$). Vehicles approaching an intersection during an active GREEN light are forced to crawl at $3.0\text{ m/s}$. Consequently, vehicles fail to reach the stop line before the signal turns RED. This wastes green phase capacity, causing queues to spill backward across upstream links.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{experiments/results/figures/figure_threshold_gating_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{Threshold-Gating Architecture Figure: Predicted congestion index $C_{\text{pred}}$ over time for light vs. peak demand. Dynamic rerouting activates selectively when $C_{\text{pred}} > 0.65$.}
\label{fig:threshold_gating}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_gating} illustrates the threshold-gating mechanism. Under light demand (\texttt{grid\_3\_light}), congestion remains below $0.65$, keeping routing dormant ($1.2$ reroutes). Under peak demand, $C_{\text{pred}}$ crosses $0.65$, triggering $1,553.8$ surgical reroutes that divert traffic to alternate grid corridors.

\section{Exploratory Guard Study}

To test whether a simple signal-aware logic guard resolves VSL interference, we implemented \texttt{vsl\_signal\_aware} (\texttt{backend/app/traffic\_manager.py:L808-L819}). The guard bypasses VSL speed reductions whenever the downstream signal displays an active green phase ('G'/'g').

This null result proves that local green-phase bypasses fail because backward-propagating spillback queues block mid-block links regardless of downstream signal phase state.

\section{Discussion \& Implications for Urban ITS}

Our findings have direct implications for smart city traffic management:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Urban-Freeway Separation}: VSL remains a freeway-context strategy. Deploying freeway VSL on urban grids without cross-layer coordination degrades performance.
    \item \textbf{Optimal Architecture}: Urban grid management should pair dynamic signal optimization (PSO) with threshold-gated routing (\texttt{signal\_and\_routing}).
    \item \textbf{Requirement for Joint Co-Optimization}: Resolving urban speed-signal interference requires continuous Model Predictive Control (MPC) or Multi-Agent RL that co-optimizes signal green splits and speed limits simultaneously.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
This paper presented a $2^3$ factorial ablation study ($N=325$ runs) evaluating signals, VSL, and routing. We proved that \texttt{signal\_and\_routing} provides optimal urban performance ($1.47\text{ m/s}$, $1,553.8$ reroutes), whereas adding uncoordinated VSL (\texttt{combined}) induces a $50\%$ performance collapse ($0.74\text{ m/s}$). Future work will focus on multi-agent reinforcement learning for joint signal-speed co-optimization.
